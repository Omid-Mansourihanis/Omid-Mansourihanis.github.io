How the model works

  • 360,000residents placed on a 600 × 600 grid, one cell per person, standing in for Sari.
  • 60,000households formed by grouping neighbouring cells at random, about six people each.
  • 15,000of them, one in four, play the role of parents’ homes.
  • 2 linksper family: its own parents (the nearest parents’ home, a Thiessen assignment) and its in-laws (a parents’ home elsewhere in the city).
  • 1,000people infected on 21 March, scattered at random. Each infects their own household first.
  1. Families visit no one except their parents or in-laws.
  2. Visits never overlap, so sibling families never meet each other directly.
  3. The virus passes only inside homes. Streets, shops and workplaces carry no infection.

Even with every visit kept separate, a parents’ home collects the virus from one family and hands it to the next. Because each family belongs to two such homes, those small clusters chain together across the whole city.

Assumptions and limits

This is an illustration of a mechanism, not a forecast. It shows how much the single parental-visit channel could spread infection on its own; it is not calibrated to reported case data for Sari.

Some timing values are assumed in this version: on a typical Nowruz day each family visits with a 60% chance (25% after the holidays), a household becomes contagious one day after infection and stays contagious for ten days, and in-laws live on average about 40 cells away. These values were chosen to roughly reproduce the original study’s reported pattern, with most new cases in the first ten days or so and most households reached within about three weeks.

Households average six people, which is larger than a typical Iranian household; the grid is a synthetic city and does not follow Sari’s real streets or neighbourhoods. The 1,000 starting cases follow the original study’s reading of Ministry of Health estimates for late March 2020.
